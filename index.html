Speed & distance

How fast the animals move and how far they get. Run speed excludes samples inside a detected reorientation, so it reflects forward locomotion only. Total distance is the length of the whole path; net displacement is the straight line from first to last position, and their ratio says how directed the movement was.

Runs

A run is the stretch of forward travel between one reorientation and the next. Its duration is the inter-reorientation interval; its distance is the path length covered over that interval. Runs cut short by the start or end of the recording are censored and excluded by default.

Navigation

Is the animal steering with respect to a gradient, or just moving? These analyses need to know where the odour (or other cue) is. Mark the source below, or switch to Set source by clicking on the Tracks tab and click the spot on a panel.

Conditions run without a gradient are the control, and they belong in the same analysis: set them to No gradient below and every measure is computed for them in exactly the same way, against the position where the cue would have been. Roaming & dwelling

On food, C. elegans alternates between two states: roaming — fast, straight, exploratory — and dwelling — slow, highly curved, staying put. Each time bin is scored from the ratio of speed to angular speed, and the fraction of time spent roaming is a compact, reproducible phenotype.

Time course

The recording is cut into equal time bins and the chosen measure is computed inside each bin, for every replicate, then averaged within a condition. This is how you see whether a difference is present from the start or develops over the assay — habituation, drying plates, a gradient forming, an animal running out of steam.

Salt gradient

A chemotaxis plate has no salt sensor in it, so the gradient has to be modelled. A drop of concentrated NaCl placed on the agar spreads by diffusion, and the concentration it leaves behind depends only on how much salt was in the drop, how long ago it was placed, how thick the agar is, and how far away you are from it. Set the plate and the drops here once and every salt analysis in the app — the response plots, the map, the exported numbers — follows from it.

The default model and its constants are the ones used in the lab's own simulation notebook, so the field you see here is the field you would get there. The exact formula, the code that runs it and a standalone Python copy are at the bottom of the Methods tab.

Plate and agar

Agar height is computed from the volume and the plate area, exactly as in the notebook: 8 mL in a 50 mm plate gives 0.4074 cm. The diffusion coefficient for NaCl is from Robinson & Stokes (1951).

Salt response

If an animal is steering by salt, its decision to reorient should depend on the salt it has just experienced — not on where it is on the plate. These plots put the reorientation rate on the y-axis and a measure of the salt change the animal just went through on the x-axis: the change in concentration over a window (ΔC), the instantaneous rate of change (dC/dt), and the change as a percentage of where it had just been.

A worm crawling down the gradient experiences negative ΔC and should turn more; crawling up it should turn less. That is the Pierce-Shimomura biased random walk, expressed in the stimulus rather than in the geometry.

What the binning and trim settings do, and when to change them

Every sample of every track carries a value of ΔC, and almost all of them are different. To plot a rate against ΔC the samples have to be gathered into groups — bins — so that each point on the line can be an honest count: the reorientations that began inside that bin, divided by the time the animals spent inside it. The two settings decide where the bin edges fall. They never change the data; they change how finely it is sliced, and a conclusion that survives both settings is one you can trust.

Equal width

The x-range is cut into bins of the same width, like a ruler. The axis is evenly spaced, which is the familiar way to read a dose–response curve and the natural thing to fit a straight line through. The cost is that the bins do not hold equal amounts of data: ΔC piles up near zero and thins out towards the extremes, so the outer bins can rest on a handful of samples and bounce around with long error bars.

Equal count

The edges are placed at quantiles, so every bin holds the same number of samples. Every point is then equally well determined — no bin is built from three samples — and nothing is discarded. The cost is an uneven x-axis: bins are narrow where the data are dense and wide out in the tails, so the spacing of the points no longer reflects the spacing of the values, and a wide outer bin is represented by a midpoint that sits nowhere in particular.

Trim (equal width only)

ΔC has a sharp peak near zero and long thin tails — a worm that happens to sprint across the steepest part of the field, or a single tracking jump, lands far out. If the bins are stretched from the true minimum to the true maximum, those few samples own most of the axis and nearly everything real is crushed into one or two bins in the middle. Trimming 1% from each tail sets the range from the 1st to the 99th percentile instead, so the bins cover the range the data actually occupy.

The price is that samples outside that range are left out of the plot altogether — their time and their reorientations both. That is fine when the tails are noise, and wrong when the tails are the biology. Set trim to 0 when the extreme ΔC values are the point of the experiment, when you need a figure that accounts for every sample, or when your distribution is compact enough that there is nothing to trim. Raise it to 2–5% when the axis still looks stretched, or when the end bins are built from almost nothing.

Equal-count binning ignores the trim box entirely: it deals with the tails by making the outer bins wide rather than by throwing them away.

In practice

Equal count is the safer default for a headline figure — nothing is discarded and no arbitrary percentage has to be defended. Equal width with a 1% trim gives the evenly spaced axis that is easier to read and to compare with published plots. The per-replicate slope is fitted across the bin midpoints, so the two settings will not give numerically identical slopes; what should hold either way is the sign and the significance. If the result flips when you switch, it is being carried by a few extreme samples, and that is worth knowing before it reaches a figure legend.

How to read each of these plots

The plots come in pairs. Each curve shows the whole group: the reorientation rate measured inside each bin of the salt variable, averaged across replicates, with error bars giving the standard error across replicates — so a short bar means the replicates agree, not that the worms were consistent. Each dot plot that follows it reduces the curve above to one number per replicate: the slope of a straight line fitted across the bins. The curve is for looking at the shape; the dot plot is what the statistics are done on.

Dashed curves are no-gradient controls, run through identical code against the position the drop would have occupied. They are the answer to the obvious objection — that the shape of the tracks alone could manufacture a relationship — and they should come out flat.

Rate vs ΔC over the window — the headline

x is how much the concentration changed over the last few seconds; negative means the animal has just moved down the gradient. A downward slope is klinokinesis, the Pierce-Shimomura biased random walk: turn more when things are getting worse, keep going when they are getting better. That alone is enough to carry a population up a gradient without any steering.

The shape matters as much as the slope. A roughly straight fall across the whole range means the animal grades its response to the size of the change. A sharp rise on the negative side with a flat positive side — the common result — means it reacts to getting worse and does little special when things improve, which is an asymmetric, threshold-like response that a single slope number flattens out. A peak at zero usually is not biology: ΔC is near zero wherever the animal is barely moving, and a stationary animal accumulates time without reorienting, or wriggles in place and trips the detector. Check the roaming and dwelling tab if the middle bin looks odd.

A slope of the opposite sign is a real result, not an error: animals conditioned at a lower salt concentration than the plate, or in avoidance assays, turn more as salt rises. The sign tells you which way the preference runs.

Rate vs ΔC over one sampling interval, and rate vs dC/dt

These two are the same measurement. dC/dt is the single-interval ΔC divided by Δt, so the curves have identical shape and the slopes differ by exactly a factor of Δt — if Δt is 1 s they are numerically identical. Both are shown because dC/dt is the quantity the sensory literature quotes and the one that goes into models of ASE and ASH, while the raw per-sample ΔC is free of any choice of window. Use the window version for the figure and these as the check: a relationship that only appears at one particular window length is a property of the window.

Rate vs % change in salt

The same change in concentration is a large event against a weak background and a small one against a strong background. This plot divides by where the animal had just been, which is Weber scaling. If it is tidier than the absolute ΔC plot — less scatter, a straighter line, tighter replicates — the animal is responding to relative rather than absolute change, which is itself a result worth reporting. Watch the far-from-the-drop bins: at low background a small absolute change is a huge percentage, so this axis stretches in exactly the region the ΔC axis compresses.

The slope dot plots

One dot per replicate — per worm, or per plate if that is your replicate unit — the bar is the group mean and the whiskers the error bar you chose. The units are rate per unit of x, so they are not comparable between the four panels; compare conditions within a panel. Two questions are being asked, and the answers are in the Statistics section below: is the slope different from zero (one-sample test, Holm-corrected — this is the one that says a response exists at all, and the one your no-gradient control should fail), and do the conditions differ from each other (the test printed on the bracket). A convincing figure has the gradient condition away from zero, the control sitting on it, and the two separated.

Three things that will mislead you

Speed is built into ΔC. A faster animal covers more ground per window and therefore reaches larger |ΔC|. If one condition is slower, its x-range is compressed and its slope can change for reasons that have nothing to do with sensing. Check the Speed tab before interpreting a slope difference, and prefer dC/dt or the percentage version if the groups differ in speed.

Where the animal was is built into ΔC too. The field is steep near the drop and flat near the rim, so large |ΔC| mostly means close to the drop. A rate difference across bins can therefore be a difference in position rather than in what was tasted. The no-gradient control and the bearing analysis on the Navigation tab are the cross-checks.

The middle bins hold most of the data. ΔC piles up near zero; the extremes are rare. Look at the per-bin counts in the exported table before believing a dramatic end point, and remember that with equal-width binning the outer bins can rest on a handful of samples.

Heading definition

The centroid track is smoothed, then re-sampled every Δt before headings are computed. Δt is the single most important knob: too small and body-bend wobble is read as turning, too large and real turns are averaged away. Pierce-Shimomura sampled at ≈1 s; 0.5–1 s is a good starting range for 30 fps video.

Statistics & plot style

A bar drawn from zero is only honest if the axis starts at zero, but when every value sits far from zero — speeds around 47, say — a zero-based axis squashes the whole distribution into a sliver. On Auto, a panel whose data occupy less than about a third of the zero-based range drops its bars and fits the axis to the data instead, and says so on the figure. Individual points and error bars are never misleading on a fitted axis, so nothing is lost.
